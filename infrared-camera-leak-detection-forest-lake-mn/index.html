Infrared Camera Leak Detection: Forest Lake, MN Homeowner Guide

An infrared camera does not see water. It sees temperature differences, and water often changes the temperature of what it touches. That makes it a useful clue-finder for leaks behind walls, but never the whole answer.

This guide explains how thermal imaging works for leak detection, where it fails, and what Forest Lake homeowners can check first.

How thermal imaging finds moisture

A thermal camera shows surface temperature as a color map. A wet section of wall or floor usually looks cooler than dry material nearby, because evaporating water pulls heat. A hot water line leaking into a slab can look warmer. The plumber looks for patterns that do not match the rest of the surface.

What thermal imaging can and cannot do
StrengthLimit
Shows temperature patterns over a large area quicklyCannot see through metal, tile backer, or thick insulation well
Finds wet insulation and drywallNeeds a temperature difference to work
Highlights warm spots on a slabDoes not confirm a leak by itself
Safe and does not damage the surfaceCan be fooled by pipes, ducts, and sunlight

Why a second method is needed

Because many things change temperature, a thermal image is a lead, not a verdict. The plumber confirms with a moisture meter, an acoustic listening device, or a pressure test on the suspect line. Only then is anything opened.

Acoustic tools listen for the sound of water escaping under pressure, and pressure testing isolates sections of pipe to show which one loses pressure. Together they narrow a leak to a small area. Leak detection explains the process.

Try this first: the meter test

Turn everything off

Close faucets and stop appliances, including the ice maker and irrigation.

Read the meter

Note the dial or the low-flow indicator.

Wait and check again

Wait 15 to 30 minutes without using water and look at the meter again.

Interpret the result

If the meter moved, water is leaking somewhere past the meter. If not, the high bill came from use.

A running toilet is the most common cause. Drop a few drops of food coloring in the tank and see if color reaches the bowl without flushing. A leaking toilet flapper is a small repair, as described under toilet repair.

Where thermal imaging helps most

  • Radiant floor heating lines, where a leak shows as a cooler or warmer patch.
  • Hot water supply lines running under a slab.
  • Wet insulation in a ceiling below a bathroom.
  • Roof and wall leaks that follow framing before they stain the paint.
  • Behind kitchen cabinets, where a slow drip may go unseen for months.

Thermal imaging is also useful for finding the edge of a wet area before drywall is cut, so the opening can be sized to the damage. That keeps repairs small and keeps costs down.

What to do while waiting for a plumber

Photograph stains with a date, note when the meter test showed movement, and move belongings away from the wet area. If you can safely turn off the main water valve, do so. Keep a list of when the symptoms started, because it helps with both the diagnosis and an insurance claim.

Who operates the street valve

According to the City of Forest Lake Water Department, the curb stop at the street is operated by city staff only. Do not count on it to stop water in an emergency, and close the main valve inside your home instead.

Forest Lake meters and bills

The City of Forest Lake requires every business or residence using city water to have a meter. Meters are read quarterly and wirelessly, so a leak can run for weeks before a bill shows it. If your bill jumps, do the meter test first.

The city also says that to find which side of the curb stop a leak is on, it shuts off the curb stop. If water keeps flowing, the leak is on the city side. If it stops, it is on the customer side. Only city employees may operate the curb stop. Homes on private wells in Scandia or Columbus have no city meter, so watch for pump cycling and pressure tank behavior instead.
